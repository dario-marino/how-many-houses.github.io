Methodology, in brief: for each ZIP code (ZCTA) in New York City and the 9-county Bay Area, we start from a current market price and current total housing units, then fit a constant-elasticity demand curve through that single observed point using an adjustable elasticity parameter (−1.5 to −0.5, spanning estimates in the literature). Set a target price and the tool shows how many additional units each ZIP would need to reach it, at your chosen elasticity.

Accounting for substitution across neighborhoods: treating every neighborhood as if it had to meet demand entirely on its own overstates how concentrated new construction would really be, since demand can shift toward nearby areas where building is easier. To address this, we draw on Baum-Snow & Han (2024), "The Microgeography of Housing Supply" (Journal of Political Economy), which finds that housing supply elasticity varies systematically within a metro area — roughly 0.2 near the urban core, rising to about 1.0 halfway to the edge and 1.2 or higher near the periphery. We compute each ZIP's distance to its metro's nearest major job center (Midtown Manhattan for New York; the nearest of San Francisco, Oakland, or San Jose for the Bay Area) and use this relationship to assign each ZIP a supply elasticity. The same total metro-wide units-needed figure implied by the independent, neighborhood-by-neighborhood model is then redistributed across ZIPs in proportion to each one's supply elasticity weighted by its own independent shortfall — so hard-to-build, high-demand areas contribute less new construction than the isolated calculation implies, and easier-to-build areas contribute more, while the metro-wide total stays fixed. Toggle between "independent" and "with substitution" above to see the difference, and both figures are shown side by side in the table below.

What this does not claim: this reallocation does not solve for a new equilibrium price in every neighborhood after construction is redistributed — that would require modeling how renters actually shift between specific ZIPs as relative prices change (a full cross-neighborhood demand substitution model), which Baum-Snow & Han also estimate (elasticities of substitution of roughly 1.5–3.5 within similar sub-areas and 3.8–8.8 between more distant areas) but which is not implemented in this tool. This model only answers where a given total construction response would concentrate if supply-side ease of building is what determines the split — a more realistic starting point than assuming every neighborhood is an island, but still a simplification.

On construction costs and building height: this tool answers "how many units are needed," not "what they would cost to build" or "what regulatory changes would be required" — both are outside its scope. For context, however, it's worth noting that the cost of adding housing supply does not rise smoothly with building height, as an early influential study (Glaeser, Gyourko & Saks, 2005) modeled it. More recent work (Cui, 2020, Real Estate Economics) finds the marginal cost of construction instead jumps at specific discontinuities — roughly an 8% jump around the 4th floor and a much larger jump (near 30% in New York) around the 8th floor, driven by elevator, fire-suppression, and structural requirements — with cost otherwise flat or even falling in between. This is provided as background only; it does not factor into the unit calculations above.

Data sources: price comes from Zillow's ZHVI (home value) and ZORI (rent) indices, both updated monthly and based on current listings/asking prices — not a blend with older, long-standing leases. Housing unit counts come from the U.S. Census Bureau's ACS 5-year estimates (2019–2023), the most current source for unit counts at this geography. A small number of low-sample ZIP codes are suppressed by Zillow and will show as "n/a."
Data: Zillow Research (ZHVI, ZORI) & U.S. Census Bureau ACS 5-Year Estimates and TIGER/Line ZCTA boundaries. Supply elasticity methodology: Baum-Snow & Han (2024), Journal of Political Economy.
