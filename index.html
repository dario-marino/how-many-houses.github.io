Methodology: for each ZIP code (ZCTA) in New York City and the 9 county Bay Area, we start from a current market price and current total housing units, then fit a constant-elasticity demand curve through that single observed point using an adjustable elasticity parameter (−1.5 to −0.5, spanning estimates in the literature). Set a target price and the tool shows how many additional units each ZIP would need to reach it, at your chosen elasticity, treating each ZIP independently. Accounting for substitution across neighborhoods: treating every neighborhood as if it had to meet demand entirely on its own overstates how concentrated new construction would really be, since demand can shift toward nearby areas where building is easier. To ground this, we draw on Baum-Snow & Han (2024), "The Microgeography of Housing Supply" (Journal of Political Economy), which finds that housing supply elasticity varies systematically within a metro area, roughly 0.2 near the urban core, rising to about 1.0 halfway to the edge and 1.2 or higher near the periphery. We compute each ZIP's distance to its metro's nearest major job center (Midtown Manhattan for New York, the nearest of San Francisco, Oakland, or San Jose for the Bay Area) and use this relationship to assign each ZIP a supply elasticity.

How the reallocation actually works: rather than pooling every ZIP's shortfall into one metro-wide bucket, each ZIP passes its excess demand to its own nearest neighbors (its six closest ZIPs by distance), which keep a share for themselves based on how easy it is to build there relative to their neighbors, and forward the rest onward — which those neighbors, in turn, again keep a share of and pass along further. Because every ZIP's outcome depends on what its neighbors do, and theirs on their own neighbors, this is a recursive system: solved exactly, in one step, via linear algebra (as a system of equations, one per ZIP, of the form T = S + M·T, solved as T = (I−M)-1S) rather than approximated by guessing at a decay rate. The metro-wide total is always preserved exactly — only where it concentrates changes. This avoids an earlier version of this model, which applied a smooth distance-decay function on top of supply elasticity (which is itself already a function of distance to the job center) and ended up double-counting distance, producing a map that looked like pure concentric rings rather than genuine variation in ease of building.

This reallocation does not solve for a new equilibrium price in every neighborhood after construction is redistributed: that would require modeling how renters actually shift between specific ZIPs as relative prices change (a full cross-neighborhood demand substitution model). This model only answers where a given total construction response would concentrate if supply side ease of building and proximity to where demand originates are what determine the split, a more realistic starting point than assuming every neighborhood is an island, but still a simplification, every model is wrong some models are useful.

This tool answers: "how many units are needed," not "what they would cost to build" or "what regulatory changes would be required". For context, it's worth noting that the cost of adding housing supply does not rise smoothly with building height, as an early study (Glaeser, Gyourko & Saks, 2005) modeled it. More recent work (Cui, 2020, Real Estate Economics) finds the marginal cost of construction instead jumps at specific discontinuities, roughly an 8% jump around the 4th floor and a much larger jump (near 30% in New York) around the 8th floor, driven by elevator, fire-suppression, and structural requirements with cost otherwise flat or even falling in between.

Data sources: price comes from Zillow's ZHVI (home value) and ZORI (rent) indices, both updated monthly and based on current listings/asking prices. Housing unit counts come from the U.S. Census Bureau's ACS 5 year estimates (2019–2023), the most current source for unit counts at this geography. A small number of low sample ZIP codes are suppressed by Zillow and will show as n/a.
